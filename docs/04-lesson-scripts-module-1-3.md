# Lesson Scripts: Modules 1–3

## Shared lesson contract

Every lesson has six visible steps: **Challenge**, **Decide**, **Consequence**, **Insight**, **Retry**, and **Real Mission**. The learner sees the challenge and options before the insight. Consequence numbers are applied to the canonical company state in `05-simulator-rules.md`. Each lesson ends with three retrieval questions; answers are checked later even if the learner gets them wrong now.

The canonical starting state is cash ₹10,00,000, monthly burn ₹83,333, runway 12 months, customers 0, churn 5%, conversion 2%, monthly revenue ₹0, morale 70/100, valuation ₹0, and evidence quality 0/100.

## Module 1 — Finding Problems

### Lesson 1.1 — Notice the friction

**Challenge (30 seconds):** “Three hostel residents complain that laundry pickup is unreliable. You have no product and ₹10,00,000 in the company. What do you do first?”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Build an app with ₹1,20,000 | Cash −₹1,20,000; burn +₹10,000/month; morale +2; evidence quality +0; customers +0 |
| B. Interview five residents about the last laundry failure | Cash −₹5,000; evidence quality +12; customer hypotheses +2; customers +0; morale +1 |
| C. Post “Would you use this?” in a college group | Cash −₹2,000; interested leads +20; evidence quality +3; conversion −0.2 percentage points; customers +0 |
| D. Offer a paid pickup tomorrow before asking questions | Cash +₹4,000 revenue; customers +2; evidence quality +5; morale +3; churn +1 percentage point due to delivery risk |

**Consequence narration:** “A complaint is a clue, not a confirmed market. Interviews cost little and increase what the company knows.”

**Insight explainer (2–3 minutes):** A problem is a repeated situation that causes a meaningful cost, delay, risk, or frustration for a defined person. A solution is what you might build. The interactive activity shows six cards; the learner drags each into **observed**, **assumed**, or **solution**. The app reveals that “laundry takes three days” is an observation, “students will pay ₹100” is an assumption, and “an app” is a solution.

**Retry:** “A resident says laundry is annoying only during exams, while a paying working professional says missed pickups cost a workday. Which person do you investigate first?” The preferred answer is the segment with repeated, costly evidence; any option is playable, but the result shows evidence quality from frequency and consequence.

**Real mission:** Speak to five people who experienced the problem in the last month. Ask what happened, what they did, what it cost, and how often it happens. Submit anonymised notes, three exact quotes, frequency count, consequence count, and a one-sentence problem statement. Valid proof is a timestamped note or audio-to-text summary with names removed; a written opinion without a conversation is invalid.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. What is the difference between a problem and a solution?
2. Which evidence is stronger: “I would use it” or a described recent workaround?
3. What four details make a problem statement specific?

### Lesson 1.2 — Choose the person

**Challenge:** “Your idea could serve students, parents, or small restaurants. You can only research one group this week. Choose.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Everyone who needs laundry | Cash −₹12,000; reachable prospects +100; evidence quality −2; conversion −1 point |
| B. Hostel students on two campuses | Cash −₹6,000; reachable prospects +35; evidence quality +10; conversion +0.5 points |
| C. Working parents in one apartment cluster | Cash −₹7,500; reachable prospects +20; evidence quality +8; conversion +0.7 points |
| D. Restaurants across the city | Cash −₹15,000; reachable prospects +50; evidence quality +2; burn +₹5,000/month |

**Insight:** A first segment is a group with a shared context and a reachable way to observe or contact them. The interactive **segment boundary** lets the learner toggle context, trigger, budget, and exclusion rules. The estimated research cost and evidence quality update live.

**Retry:** A campus segment has 35 prospects but only 8 experienced the problem this week. A city segment has 50 prospects but no shared context. Select the segment that makes the next test observable and explain the exclusion rule.

**Real mission:** Write inclusion and exclusion rules for one segment, then recruit five people matching them. Valid proof includes the criteria, recruitment channel, and anonymised confirmation that each person matches; screenshots of a broad poll alone do not count.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. Why is “everyone” not a useful first segment?
2. What is an exclusion rule?
3. When is a smaller segment strategically stronger?

### Lesson 1.3 — Measure the pain

**Challenge:** “You heard four complaints. Which problem should receive your next ₹20,000?”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Frequent but mildly annoying late delivery | Cash −₹20,000; evidence quality +8; customers +1; conversion +0.2 points |
| B. Rare but expensive missed pickup | Cash −₹20,000; evidence quality +10; customers +2; conversion +0.8 points |
| C. Easy-to-build tracking screen | Cash −₹20,000; evidence quality +2; morale +2; customers +0 |
| D. The loudest complaint online | Cash −₹20,000; evidence quality +4; interested leads +20; churn +0.5 points |

**Insight:** Score each problem from 1–5 for frequency, severity, and workaround dissatisfaction. The interactive matrix lets the learner move each problem; total score is `frequency × severity + workaround`. The app shows that a rare but costly problem may be worth testing, while an easy feature is not automatically valuable.

**Retry:** “Problem A scores frequency 5, severity 2, workaround 1. Problem B scores frequency 2, severity 5, workaround 5. Which gets a validation test, and what uncertainty remains?”

**Real mission:** Ask each interviewee to score frequency, severity, and current workaround from 1–5, then calculate a ranked table. Valid proof requires at least five responses and a note explaining one disagreement.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. Why should frequency and severity both be measured?
2. What does a workaround reveal?
3. What uncertainty remains after a high score?

### Lesson 1.4 — Frame the opportunity

**Challenge:** “Choose the strongest statement for your next test.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. “We will revolutionise laundry for India.” | Evidence quality +0; conversion −0.5 points; morale +1 |
| B. “Hostel students in Pune lose 2–3 hours weekly chasing delayed laundry pickups.” | Evidence quality +10; conversion +0.5 points; morale +2 |
| C. “We will build a pickup marketplace.” | Evidence quality +1; cash −₹10,000; customers +0 |
| D. “Everyone hates waiting.” | Evidence quality +2; conversion −0.2 points; customers +0 |

**Insight:** Use the frame: “For [specific person] in [context], [problem] happens [frequency], causing [consequence]. We believe [testable change] will improve [metric].” The learner fills each slot with cards, then marks each claim as observed or assumed.

**Retry:** The learner rewrites a broad statement using their strongest interview quote and chooses one measurable outcome.

**Real mission:** Submit a one-page opportunity brief: segment, problem frame, three supporting quotes, one contradiction, and the next test metric. Valid proof is traceable to the five interviews.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. Which part of the frame must be observable?
2. Why label assumptions?
3. What makes an opportunity brief actionable?

## Module 2 — Knowing Your Customer

### Lesson 2.1 — Segment, do not stereotype

**Challenge:** “A message says ‘young people hate chores.’ Choose who you will actually study.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. All young people | Cash −₹8,000; evidence quality +1; conversion −0.8 points |
| B. First-year hostel students sharing a laundry room | Cash −₹5,000; evidence quality +11; conversion +0.6 points |
| C. Busy professionals who own washing machines | Cash −₹7,000; evidence quality +5; conversion +0.4 points |
| D. People who complain about anything | Cash −₹3,000; evidence quality −2; interested leads +15 |

**Insight:** A segment is defined by a shared situation and reachable behaviour, not a demographic label alone. The interactive builder makes the learner select context, trigger, current workaround, and access channel; the confidence meter falls when a claim lacks evidence.

**Retry:** Pick a segment that can be reached twice in one week and write one exclusion rule.

**Real mission:** Recruit five matching participants and document how each meets the boundary. Valid proof is participant criteria plus anonymised recruitment evidence.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. What makes a segment reachable?
2. Why can a demographic label be insufficient?
3. What does an exclusion rule protect?

### Lesson 2.2 — Map the moment

**Challenge:** “A customer says, ‘Laundry is fine most days.’ Find the moment worth understanding.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Ask them to rate the idea 1–10 | Evidence quality +2; conversion +0.1 points |
| B. Ask what happened the last time the service failed | Evidence quality +10; customer hypotheses +1 |
| C. Show a polished app mock-up | Cash −₹15,000; evidence quality +3; morale +2 |
| D. Ask whether they would recommend it | Evidence quality +4; referral rate +0.5 points |

**Insight:** A journey map records trigger, action, friction, workaround, desired outcome, and consequence. The learner drags a real example across the six stages; missing evidence is shown as a blank, not filled by AI.

**Retry:** Compare a “mild annoyance every week” journey with a “missed exam uniform” journey. Select the moment with both repeated behaviour and meaningful consequence.

**Real mission:** Map one participant’s last real episode and verify the timeline with follow-up questions. Valid proof includes exact sequence and a quote at the friction point.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. What is a trigger?
2. Why is a last episode more useful than a general rating?
3. What belongs in a workaround?

### Lesson 2.3 — Ask and listen

**Challenge:** “You have ten minutes. Choose the first question.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. “Would you pay ₹99 for our app?” | Evidence quality +1; leading-question penalty +2 |
| B. “Tell me about the last time this happened.” | Evidence quality +10; customer hypotheses +1 |
| C. “Do you agree this is a big problem?” | Evidence quality +2; leading-question penalty +1 |
| D. “What features should we build?” | Evidence quality +3; solution bias +2 |

**Insight:** Neutral interviews ask about the past, behaviour, frequency, consequence, and workaround. The interactive roleplay pauses after each question and highlights leading words. The learner rewrites one leading question before continuing.

**Retry:** The customer gives a vague answer. The learner chooses a follow-up: “What happened next?”, “How did you solve it?”, or “Would you use an app?” Only the first two add behavioural evidence.

**Real mission:** Conduct one 10-minute interview using five neutral questions. Valid proof is anonymised notes with at least one past episode, workaround, and consequence; invented answers are not valid.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. Why ask about the last time?
2. What makes a question leading?
3. Which follow-up uncovers behaviour?

### Lesson 2.4 — Turn evidence into hypotheses

**Challenge:** “Five interviews disagree about price. What do you write down?”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Average the answers and call it truth | Evidence quality −3; conversion +0.1 points |
| B. Record the disagreement and define a price test | Evidence quality +9; conversion +0.3 points |
| C. Ignore price until launch | Evidence quality +0; cash −₹25,000 on later rework |
| D. Ask an AI to choose the price | Evidence quality −2; morale −1; cash −₹2,000 |

**Insight:** A hypothesis states audience, behaviour, threshold, and timebox: “We believe [segment] will [behaviour] at [threshold] within [time].” The interactive table lets the learner tag evidence as repeated, contradictory, or missing and choose confidence.

**Retry:** Turn “people liked the idea” into a measurable hypothesis using a payment or commitment behaviour.

**Real mission:** Submit three hypotheses, each linked to evidence and a next test. Valid proof includes the contradiction and why the confidence level is not 100%.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. What four parts make a hypothesis testable?
2. Why preserve contradictions?
3. What is stronger than stated interest?

## Module 3 — Validating the Idea

### Lesson 3.1 — Find the riskiest assumption

**Challenge:** “Your venture assumes customers have the problem, will switch, and will pay. Which test comes first?”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Test the logo colour | Cash −₹5,000; evidence quality +1 |
| B. Test whether the problem occurs weekly | Cash −₹8,000; evidence quality +9 |
| C. Build every requested feature | Cash −₹1,50,000; burn +₹12,000/month; evidence quality +2 |
| D. Run a broad awareness campaign | Cash −₹40,000; interested leads +100; evidence quality +3 |

**Insight:** Rank assumptions by impact if false and uncertainty. The interactive risk matrix places each assumption; the highest-impact/lowest-confidence card becomes the next test. Learners see that a beautiful solution cannot rescue a false problem.

**Retry:** Move “customers will switch from their workaround” above “customers like our name,” then choose a test that observes switching.

**Real mission:** Write a risk register with five assumptions, impact/confidence scores, and the single riskiest assumption. Valid proof includes why the chosen test could change the venture decision.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. What makes an assumption risky?
2. Why is a logo rarely the first risk?
3. What evidence can show switching?

### Lesson 3.2 — Design a fair test

**Challenge:** “Choose a validation plan for a hostel laundry service.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Ask 100 people if they like it | Cash −₹3,000; leads +100; evidence quality +3 |
| B. Offer a paid pilot to 10 matching residents | Cash −₹12,000; customers +4; evidence quality +14 |
| C. Build for two months before showing anyone | Cash −₹1,00,000; burn +₹8,000/month; evidence quality +4 |
| D. Give a free service to friends | Cash −₹6,000; customers +5; evidence quality +5; willingness-to-pay confidence −1 |

**Insight:** A fair test pre-declares audience, behaviour, threshold, timebox, and interpretation. The interactive test card prevents changing the threshold after results are entered.

**Retry:** Set “4 of 10 pay a deposit within 7 days” before seeing results. If 3 pay, the app asks whether to pivot the segment or run a second test; it does not label the idea a failure.

**Real mission:** Run a test with a written threshold and timebox. Valid proof includes the pre-dated plan, anonymised result, and decision memo.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. Why pre-declare a threshold?
2. What is the difference between interest and commitment?
3. What makes a test fair?

### Lesson 3.3 — Read signals

**Challenge:** “Your landing page gets 200 visits, 40 sign-ups, and 2 paid deposits. What do you conclude?”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Declare product-market fit | Evidence quality −5; valuation +₹50,000 temporarily; later churn +3 points |
| B. Celebrate interest, investigate payment friction | Evidence quality +8; conversion +0.4 points |
| C. Ignore the sign-ups | Evidence quality +0; morale −2 |
| D. Spend ₹80,000 on more traffic | Cash −₹80,000; visits +500; evidence quality +1 |

**Insight:** The funnel separates awareness, interest, activation, conversion, retention, and referral. The interactive funnel lets the learner click each stage and see the percentage and uncertainty. The app distinguishes stated intent from observed behaviour.

**Retry:** Compare 10/20 paid deposits with 100/1,000 free sign-ups. The first is stronger evidence of willingness to pay, though sample size and context still matter.

**Real mission:** Build a five-stage funnel from one test, label each number’s source, and identify the biggest uncertainty. Valid proof is source-tagged analytics or a manually auditable count.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. Which signal is closest to payment evidence?
2. Why can a large sign-up number mislead?
3. What is a funnel leak?

### Lesson 3.4 — Make the call

**Challenge:** “Your test missed its threshold but revealed that apartment residents, not hostel students, have the strongest need. Choose the next move.”

**Decision options and exact consequences:**

| Option | Consequence |
|---|---|
| A. Persevere with the same segment and message | Cash −₹15,000; evidence quality +3; morale −1 |
| B. Pivot segment and run a focused second test | Cash −₹20,000; evidence quality +12; customers +2; morale +1 |
| C. Stop the idea and preserve cash | Cash +₹0; burn −₹5,000/month; morale +0; learning score +8 |
| D. Hide the result from the team | Evidence quality −8; morale −5; churn +2 points |

**Insight:** A decision memo records the prediction, result, interpretation, next action, and what would disprove it. The interactive memo forces the learner to choose one of persevere, pivot, or stop and shows the cash/runway consequence.

**Retry:** Write a pivot that changes one variable only (segment, problem, channel, or solution) so learning remains attributable.

**Real mission:** Submit the test report and decision memo to a peer or facilitator. Valid proof includes the original threshold, raw result, and a changed next step.

**Retrieval practice:**
1. What is a pivot?
2. Why change one variable at a time?
3. How can stopping be a learning success?
